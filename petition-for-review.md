# Motion for Rehearing → Petition for Review

*Converting error-correction into review-worthiness.*

A motion for rehearing and a petition for review share raw material and make opposite arguments. Rehearing says **the panel got it wrong.** A petition must say **why the error matters beyond this case** — because a court of discretionary review selects cases for their importance, not their wrongness.

Most petitions fail at exactly this seam: they are the rehearing motion with a new cover. This prompt performs the reframing systematically, argument by argument, and forces the decision to drop an argument to be made deliberately rather than discovered at the word limit.

## The prompt

```
Using the [APPELLATE OPINION — the intermediate court's opinion and judgment,
including any substitute opinion issued on rehearing; the petition attacks this
document, not the trial court's], the [MOTION FOR REHEARING — the motion (and any
en banc motion) whose arguments are the raw material being converted; it marks
exactly where the panel's reasoning was challenged], the [APPELLATE BRIEFS — both
sides' merits briefs below; they supply the record citations and preserve the scope
of what may be raised], and any [SEPARATE WRITINGS — dissents or concurrences from
the panel or from denial of en banc review; a dissent is itself evidence the
question is review-worthy] in this folder, draft a petition for review to
[REVIEWING COURT — e.g., the Supreme Court of Texas] on behalf of [CLIENT PARTY —
the party our firm represents, now petitioner].

The core transformation: for each argument in the motion for rehearing, reframe it
from error-correction ('the panel misapplied X') into review-worthiness under
[REVIEW-GRANTING STANDARD — the factors the reviewing court uses to select cases,
e.g., Tex. R. App. P. 56.1(a): conflict among the courts of appeals, construction
of a statute or constitutional provision, questions of first impression, or error of
such importance to the state's jurisprudence that it should be corrected].

Produce a conversion table FIRST: each rehearing argument; the review factor(s) it
can honestly claim; the reframed issue statement; and — where an argument cannot be
reframed to any factor — an explicit recommendation to drop it, with the reason. Do
not carry an argument forward just because the rehearing motion made it.

Then draft the petition in the structure required by [PETITION RULE — the rule
governing petition contents, e.g., Tex. R. App. P. 53.2]: identity of parties and
counsel; statement of the case; statement of jurisdiction tying each issue to a
specific statutory basis under [JURISDICTIONAL STATUTE — e.g., Tex. Gov't Code
§ 22.001]; issues presented; statement of facts; summary of the argument; argument;
prayer; and the appendix list.

Limit the petition to [NUMBER — two or three] issues. Each issue must be framed so
the reason the court should take the case is visible in the question itself, not
deferred to the argument section.

Write the statement of the case and statement of facts for a court that has not
lived with this case: short, neutral in tone, and pointed toward the legal question
rather than the grievance. Every record and opinion citation in [CITATION CONVENTION
— the citation format used in the briefs below, e.g., Greenbook form with CR/RR
record cites]. Anything you cannot verify against the documents in the folder goes
on a verification checklist at the end, not silently into the draft.

Observe the [WORD LIMIT — the petition word cap, e.g., 4,500 words for a Texas
petition for review, exclusive of the sections the rule excludes]; report the word
count by section at the end.

Before reading anything at length, inventory the folder against the document list
above and tell me what is missing — most commonly the substitute opinion on
rehearing or a separate writing. Estimate the total reading cost and ask my approval
before any large read. Present the conversion table and the draft in chat for my
review before saving anything; after approval, save as a Word document in the
folder.
```

## Guardrails — behaviours to demand if they don't happen automatically

1. **Reframe, don't recaption.** The classic failure is a petition that is the rehearing motion with a new cover: pure error-correction. The reviewing court selects cases for their importance, not their wrongness — every surviving argument must answer *why should this court, of all courts, care.* **If a draft section reads as an appeal re-argued, send it back.**
2. **The conversion table comes first.** Requiring the argument-by-argument table before any drafting exposes which rehearing points have no review factor to stand on. The decision to drop them is then made deliberately by the attorney, not discovered at the word limit.
3. **A dissent is an exhibit, not a footnote.** Separate writings and en banc votes are the strongest available evidence of review-worthiness. If one exists it belongs in the issues presented and the summary, quoted — not buried in the argument.
4. **Jurisdiction is argued, not recited.** The statement of jurisdiction must connect each issue to its specific statutory basis and say why, in a sentence or two. A bare citation to the jurisdictional statute is a missed opportunity opposing counsel will not make.
5. **Word-limit triage is the point.** The cap is a feature: it forces the strongest two or three issues. If the draft arrives over the limit, **the fix is cutting issues, not compressing prose into unreadability.** Demand the per-section word count with every draft.
6. **Fresh eyes on the facts.** The statement of facts must be rebuilt for a cold reader, not pasted from the merits brief below. The petition audience knows nothing and will not forgive being made to feel it.

---

## Style points for the deliverable

**Importance-first framing in every section** — the first sentence of the summary should state what the case means for the jurisprudence, not what happened to the client. Issues presented in the deep-issue style: premise, premise, question, each under seventy-five words where possible.

The conversion table ships with the draft as a separate section, so the reasoning behind dropped arguments is preserved. Petition voice is confident and forward-looking, never aggrieved — the panel below *erred*; it was not *outrageous*. Appendix list complete against the rule's requirements. Work-product legend on every page.
